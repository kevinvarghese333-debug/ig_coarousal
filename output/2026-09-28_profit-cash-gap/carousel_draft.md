## Strategic Premise

- Topic: Why a company can report profit while its cash position is deteriorating.
- Central idea: Profit is booked the moment a sale is invoiced; cash is only real when it lands in the account. Growth widens that gap instead of closing it.
- Primary audience: Business-curious Indian professionals aged 21-40 who follow company news and consider themselves investors or aspiring investors, but read "profitable" as a finished conclusion rather than one data point.
- Audience tension: A "profitable" headline feels safe and final, but it hides a timing gap that can sink an otherwise healthy-looking company.
- Primary engagement goal: Saves (this is a checklist-style investor habit worth returning to) and comments from people naming companies or situations where they have seen this play out.
- Why people may save, share or comment: It reframes a familiar business-news pattern (profit headline, then a cash crisis) with a clear mechanism, gives a concrete rule to apply before trusting any "profitable" claim, and flatters the reader's ability to spot something others miss.

## Five Cover Hooks

1. Curiosity hook: "The numbers said profit. The bank account disagreed."
2. Contrarian hook: "A 'profitable' company can still run out of money."
3. Relatable-pain hook: "You have seen this headline before: profit, then layoffs."
4. Hidden-business-mechanism hook: "Here is what a profit headline does not tell you."
5. Warning-or-risk hook: "The company is growing. Its cash is missing."

**Selected hook:** The company is growing. Its cash is missing.

**Why it wins:** It sets up an immediate contradiction (growth should mean more cash, not less) that only makes sense once the mechanism is explained, which earns the swipe without needing a scare word.

## Caption

Every few months, a growing company makes headlines for record profit. A few months later comes news of layoffs or a cash scramble. The two stories do not contradict each other. They describe two different numbers.

Profit is booked the moment a sale is invoiced. Cash is counted only when it actually lands in the account. A company can log a sale today and wait 60, 90, even 120 days to be paid for it. For that whole stretch, the business is profitable on paper and short on cash in the bank.

Growth does not fix this. It usually makes it worse, since more sales mean more unpaid invoices sitting in the system at once.

This is not a reason to distrust every growing business. It is a reason to look past one number. Profit tells you the story a company wants to show. The cash flow statement tells you if that story is actually funded.

Have you ever checked a company's cash flow statement before trusting its profit number?
