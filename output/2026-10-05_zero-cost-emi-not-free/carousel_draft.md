# Zero-cost EMI is not always free

## Status

Production complete. Review before Instagram publishing.

## Strategic Premise

- Topic: No-cost EMI
- Central idea: "No-cost" EMI is not automatically free, because the interest cost can move into the price, a fee or the habit of buying.
- Primary audience: Indian salaried buyers aged 21 to 40 who use card and checkout EMIs
- Audience tension: It felt free, so why check?
- Primary engagement goal: Saves and comments
- Why people may save, share or comment: A three-question checklist before the next sale, and a relatable judgement question.

## Five Cover Hooks

1. Curiosity hook: Who pays for your no-cost EMI?
2. Contrarian hook: Zero-cost EMI is not always free.
3. Relatable-pain hook: You tapped no-cost EMI. Did you check the cash price?
4. Hidden-business-mechanism hook: The interest did not vanish. It became a discount.
5. Warning-or-risk hook: Before you tap no-cost EMI, read this.

**Selected hook:** Zero-cost EMI is not always free. The cost just moves.

**Why it wins:** It corrects a belief most checkout users hold, in under one second, with one focal point.

## Caption

"No-cost EMI" sounds like the lender is working for free. It is not. Interest is a real cost of lending money, so the cost has to be paid by someone, somewhere.

Sometimes it sits in the price: the cash price is lower, but the EMI price is not. Sometimes it shows up as a fee. Sometimes it is built into a list price that everyone pays.

RBI's credit-card rules ask issuers to show the principal, interest and upfront discount clearly when a purchase is converted to EMI, and not to disguise an interest component as zero. Terms differ by card, lender and merchant, so check the current terms before you tap.

The part I find more interesting is behavioural. A big price turns into a small monthly number, and the decision quietly changes from "Can I afford this?" to "Can I manage the monthly?"

Save this for the next big sale.
