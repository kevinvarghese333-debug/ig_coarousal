# Zero-Cost EMI Is Not Always a Free Decision

## Status

Production complete. Review before Instagram publishing.

## Strategic Premise

- Topic: Zero-cost / "No Cost" EMI on retail purchases (phones, appliances, electronics)
- Central idea: "No Cost EMI" removes the interest line from the checkout screen, not the cost itself — the cost usually reappears as a forfeited cash discount, a processing fee, or GST charged on interest the bank still books internally.
- Primary audience: Indian salaried professionals and first-time buyers, 21 to 40, who use EMI at checkout for phones, gadgets and appliances
- Audience tension: They believe "No Cost" literally means free, and tap it as the default choice without comparing it to paying in full
- Primary engagement goal: Saves and shares (this is a decision checklist people will want to reference next time they are at checkout, and forward to a friend who is about to buy on EMI)
- Why people may save, share or comment: It corrects a belief nearly everyone holds, gives a concrete 3-step check to run before tapping the EMI box, and ends with a genuine judgement question (lower EMI vs. paying in full) that invites a real answer, not just a reaction

## Five Cover Hooks

1. Curiosity hook: "The zero in 'No Cost EMI' is not what it looks like."
2. Contrarian hook: "Zero-cost EMI is not always a free decision."
3. Relatable-pain hook: "You picked 'No Cost EMI' to save money. That was the assumption to check."
4. Hidden-business-mechanism hook: "Someone always pays the bank's interest. It is rarely the bank."
5. Warning-or-risk hook: "RBI called out 'No Cost EMI' once already. The label never really changed."

**Selected hook:** Zero-cost EMI is not always a free decision.

**Why it wins:** It is a contrarian correction in the reader's own checkout vocabulary, creates instant tension without fear-mongering, and sets up a carousel people finish because they now need to know where the "zero" actually went.

## Caption

That "No Cost EMI" button at checkout is doing more work than it lets on. Banks do not lend for free, so when a screen shows zero percent interest, the interest has not disappeared. It has usually moved.

Sometimes it shows up as a cash discount you would have got for paying upfront, quietly removed the moment you choose EMI. Sometimes it shows up as a processing fee. Sometimes it is GST charged on interest the bank still records internally, even though your bill calls it "no cost."

RBI flagged this pattern back in 2013, calling the zero percent framing "camouflaged." The label changed from "zero percent" to "no cost." The mechanism mostly did not.

None of this means EMI is wrong for you. It means "no cost" is a marketing line, not a receipt. Before you tap that box, run the three-step check on slide 8 and see what the actual gap is.

Would you choose the lower EMI, or wait and pay in full for the discount? Genuinely curious where people land on this.
