# No Cost EMI Is Not Actually Free

## Status

Production complete. Review before Instagram publishing.

## Strategic Premise

- Topic: The hidden mechanism behind "No Cost EMI" offers on retail and e-commerce purchases in India.
- Central idea: No Cost EMI does not remove the interest. It moves the cost into a forfeited discount, a loaded price, or a separate fee, and RBI has been saying so since 2013.
- Primary audience: Salaried, 21 to 40, first-time and repeat online shoppers who convert purchases to EMI without comparing it to the cash price.
- Audience tension: The checkout screen makes "No Cost EMI" feel like a free upgrade, not a financial decision.
- Primary engagement goal: Saves and shares (a decision checklist people want to reuse before their next big purchase) plus comments from people who have never checked the cash price.
- Why people may save, share or comment: It reframes a purchase they make often, gives a concrete mechanism ("where did the discount go"), and ends with a checklist worth revisiting.

## Five Cover Hooks

1. Curiosity hook: "The 'No Cost EMI' button hides one simple question."
2. Contrarian hook: "Zero percent interest does not exist. RBI said so in 2013."
3. Relatable-pain hook: "You picked No Cost EMI. Somebody still paid the interest."
4. Hidden-business-mechanism hook: "No Cost EMI is not actually free. The cost just moves."
5. Warning-or-risk hook: "That 'No Cost' tag can quietly cost you your discount."

**Selected hook:** No Cost EMI is not actually free. The cost just moves somewhere you don't see it.

**Why it wins:** It corrects a belief nearly every reader holds, in plain language, without sounding alarmist or guru-ish.

## Caption

No Cost EMI is one of the most persuasive lines in Indian retail. Zero percent interest, easy checkout, approval in three taps. It works because it sounds like the bank gave up its usual cut.

It did not. RBI flagged this as far back as 2013, telling banks that zero percent interest does not really exist and that the cost was simply being folded into a fee instead of a rate. The mechanism has not changed much since. Either the cash discount you would have earned quietly disappears, or the price is loaded before the EMI is calculated.

None of this means No Cost EMI is a trap every time. It means the label is doing marketing work, not accounting work. The number worth checking is the APR, the all-in cost, which RBI now requires lenders to disclose upfront in a Key Facts Statement.

Have you ever compared the cash price against the EMI price on the same purchase, or did the banner make that comparison feel unnecessary?
