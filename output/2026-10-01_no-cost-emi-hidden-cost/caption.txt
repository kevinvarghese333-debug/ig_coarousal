"No-cost EMI" sounds like the shop is lending you money for free. Usually, the cost has just moved.

Depending on the seller and the bank, it can show up as a cash or UPI discount you no longer get, as interest that is covered upfront and recovered inside the deal, or as a fee billed separately. The slides use an illustrative example: a phone at ₹57,000 if you pay today and ₹60,000 across six "no-cost" EMIs. The ₹3,000 gap works out to roughly 18% a year. Real offers differ, so the numbers here are only to show the method.

RBI's Master Direction on credit cards says card EMI conversions must show principal, interest and the upfront discount before conversion, and again on the statement, and that interest should not be camouflaged as no-cost.

The habit worth keeping is simple: ask for the pay-today price, then compare it with the total of all EMIs.
