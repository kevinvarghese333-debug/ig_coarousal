"No-cost EMI" sounds like a free loan. Usually it is not.

When a seller offers an instalment plan with no visible interest, the cost can still be there. It may sit in a cash discount you lose by choosing EMI, in a processing fee, or in a higher listed price. The terms differ by lender, card and seller, so check the current terms for your own offer.

In the illustrative example in this carousel, a phone costs ₹57,000 if you pay today and ₹60,000 across six "no-cost" instalments. Those numbers are assumed, but the logic is real: the gap between the two prices is the price of borrowing.

None of this means EMI is bad. Spreading a payment can be useful. The point is to know what you are paying for the convenience before you tap Buy.

Next time you see "no-cost EMI", ask for the pay-today price and the full list of charges.
