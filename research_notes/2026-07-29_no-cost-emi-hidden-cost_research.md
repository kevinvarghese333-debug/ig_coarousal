# Research Notes: Why "No-Cost EMI" Is Not a Free Decision

## Topic Selected

Why zero-cost / "no-cost" EMI is not always a free decision — the interest does not disappear, it moves to a place the buyer cannot see as easily (price, forgone discount, or GST on the finance component).

## Why It Was Selected

- Priority topic #1 in `carousel_topic_bank.md`, and no carousel has been published or drafted yet (log and topic bank are both empty of history), so there is no repeat-topic conflict.
- Extremely high relevance to the primary audience: salaried professionals aged 21-40 buying phones, appliances, gadgets on EMI, especially around festive/sale season.
- Strong save/share potential: it is a decision framework ("check these three things before saying yes to no-cost EMI"), which the mastermind doc flags as a save-driver.
- Backed by an actual, checkable regulatory action (RBI, 2013), not just a general behavioural claim, which keeps the account's "informed by the real economy" positioning intact.
- Matches Carousel Type 3 (The Trap or Warning): appealing surface, missing question, risk, safer way to think.

## Primary and Secondary Sources Consulted

1. **RBI action on zero percent EMI schemes, September 2013** (via Business Standard/PTI/Reuters wire reporting, since the original RBI press release/circular PDF could not be directly located from this environment).
   - Business Standard: "RBI asks banks to charge uniform interest rate on retail EMI schemes" (Sept 2013) — https://www.business-standard.com/amp/article/reuters/rbi-asks-banks-to-charge-uniform-interest-rate-on-retail-emi-schemes-113092500642_1.html
   - Business Standard: "RBI bans zero per cent interest rate scheme for buying goods" (PTI, Sept 2013) — https://www.business-standard.com/amp/article/pti-stories/rbi-bans-zero-per-cent-interest-rate-scheme-for-buying-goods-113092500786_1.html
   - Business Standard: "RBI cracks whip on retail finance schemes with hidden cost" (Sept 2013) — https://www.business-standard.com/article/finance/rbi-cracks-whip-on-retail-finance-schemes-with-hidden-cost-113092500706_1.html
   - Reported quote attributed to RBI: "Since the very concept of zero percent interest is non-existent and fair practice demands that the processing charge and RoI charged should be kept uniform product/segment wise... such schemes only serve the purpose of alluring and exploiting the vulnerable customers."
   - Reported mechanism: the interest element is "camouflaged" and passed on to customers as processing fees or built into the sticker price via an opaque dealer discount retained by the bank.
   - Status: **[VERIFY]** — this is wire-service/business-press reporting of an RBI communication from 2013, not a direct link to the RBI circular PDF. Kevin should locate and cite the original RBI notification/circular number before publishing, or keep the claim at the level of "in 2013 the RBI publicly stated..." sourced to contemporaneous reporting.

2. **How the cost is actually absorbed today** (aggregator/explainer sources, not primary — used only for the general mechanism, not for any specific number):
   - Paisabazaar — "No-Cost EMI on Credit Cards: Key Things to Know" — https://www.paisabazaar.com/credit-card/need-know-no-cost-emi-credit-cards/
   - ClearTax — "GST on No Cost EMI: Rates, Applicability and Credit Card Impact" — https://cleartax.in/s/gst-on-no-cost-emi
   - Kotak Mahindra AMC — "No Cost EMI Explained: Hidden Charges, Real Cost & Smart Buying Tips" — https://www.kotakmf.com/Information/blogs/economics-behind-no-cost-emi
   - Common mechanism described across these sources: the interest cost is covered either (a) by the merchant/brand giving up the cash discount it would otherwise have offered, or (b) by pricing the product slightly higher than the cash price, so the "0%" line is true only against an already-inflated base price.
   - Status: general market mechanism, consistent across multiple independent explainer sources — used in the carousel as an explained mechanism, not attributed to a specific bank, lender or retailer.

3. **GST on the interest/finance component**
   - ClearTax — https://cleartax.in/s/gst-on-no-cost-emi — states GST (the standard financial-services rate) applies to the interest/finance component and to any processing fee, even when the sticker price shows "0% interest."
   - Status: **[VERIFY]** — the exact GST treatment can depend on how a specific bank or NBFC structures the scheme (interest waiver vs. subvention vs. cashback-after-interest). The carousel uses this only as a general "GST can apply to the finance charge" caution, without quoting a specific rupee figure, and recommends the reader check the current terms on their own statement.

4. **Processing fees**
   - Multiple explainer sources mention processing fees in a wide range (reported figures vary source to source, e.g., roughly ₹99 to ₹500 depending on lender and product).
   - Status: **[VERIFY] — EXCLUDED FROM ON-SLIDE COPY.** This number is fragile and lender-dependent, which the production brief explicitly flags as a reason to leave it out of slide copy. The carousel refers to "a processing fee" qualitatively and tells the reader to check their own terms, without citing a rupee figure.

## Claims Included in the Carousel (as of final draft)

- "No-cost EMI" is a marketing label. In 2013, the RBI publicly said zero percent interest does not really exist and that the cost is camouflaged as a processing fee or built into price. [Slide 4/6] — sourced to 2013 wire reporting, flagged [VERIFY] for the primary circular.
- The cost is typically absorbed in one of two ways: the merchant gives up the cash discount, or the "0%" price is calculated on a slightly higher base price. [Slide 4] — general market mechanism from multiple explainer sources, phrased with "often"/"can" rather than as universal fact.
- GST can still apply to the interest/finance component even when the interest line reads "0%." [Slide 6] — phrased as "can apply," reader told to check current terms; no specific rupee or percentage figure used on-slide.
- Illustrative worked example on Slide 5 (a ₹40,000 phone, cash price vs EMI price) is a **hypothetical arithmetic illustration built for teaching purposes**, not a claim about any real retailer, bank or product. It is clearly presented as an example, not as reported fact, so it does not require source verification, but this is noted here for transparency.
