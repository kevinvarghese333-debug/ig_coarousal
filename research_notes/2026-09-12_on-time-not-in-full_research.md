# Research Notes: Credit Card "On Time" vs "In Full"

## Topic Selected

Why people who pay their credit-card bill on time can still get into money trouble, because paying "on time" is not the same as paying "in full."

## Research Notes

- The core mechanism: a credit card's interest-free period (typically 20 to 50 days depending on the bank and the transaction date within the cycle) applies only when the cardholder clears the ENTIRE "Total Amount Due" by the due date. Paying only the "Minimum Amount Due," or any amount less than the full total, forfeits the interest-free period for that entire billing cycle.
- Once forfeited, interest is charged from the original transaction date on the outstanding balance, not from the due date and not only on the unpaid sliver. This means a cardholder who pays, say, 90 percent of the bill on time can still be charged interest on the full cycle's spend, calculated from each purchase date.
- This is a genuine "on time but not in full" trap: a person can feel responsible (paid before the due date, never missed a payment) while still paying meaningful interest, because they paid an amount rather than the full amount.
- No specific interest rate, exact grace-period day count, or rupee example is used on-slide, since these vary by bank and product and were not confirmed against a specific issuer's current terms this run. The carousel describes the mechanism and rule, not a specific numeric cost.
- The word "credit score" is intentionally not used as the main hook for this carousel (that is a separate, already-used priority topic: "What a credit score does not tell a lender") to keep this carousel's central idea narrow and undiluted.

## Claims Included in the Carousel

1. The interest-free period on a credit card applies only if the entire Total Amount Due is paid by the due date. Paying anything less, including the Minimum Amount Due, forfeits it for that cycle. Confidence: high, direct from RBI's own FAQ.
2. Once the interest-free period is lost, interest is charged from the date of each transaction on the outstanding balance, not just from the due date onward. Confidence: high, direct from RBI's own FAQ.
3. A cardholder can pay before the due date every month and still pay interest, because paying on time and paying in full are different things. Confidence: high, this is the direct logical consequence of claims 1 and 2, not an independent claim needing separate sourcing.

## Claims Removed Because They Were Not Adequately Verified

- Any specific interest rate (for example, "3.5% per month") was excluded, since credit card interest rates vary meaningfully by issuer and card product and none was confirmed against a specific, current bank document this run.
- Any specific grace-period day count (for example, "up to 50 days") was excluded from on-slide copy for the same reason; the copy refers to "the interest-free period" generically instead of naming a day count.
- Any claim about a specific bank's current Minimum Amount Due formula (commonly cited informally as roughly 5 percent of the outstanding balance plus fees and EMI components) was excluded, since this varies by issuer and was not confirmed against a specific bank's current terms and conditions this run.
