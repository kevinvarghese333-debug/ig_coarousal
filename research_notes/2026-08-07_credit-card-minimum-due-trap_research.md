# Research Notes: The "On Time" Credit Card Trap

## Topic selected

Why people who pay their credit card bill "on time" every month can still be paying interest on their entire spend, not just the part they did not pay.

## Why it was selected

- Priority topic #2 in `carousel_topic_bank.md`: "Why people who pay their credit-card bill on time still get into money trouble." Not previously drafted or published (log and topic bank are both empty).
- High confusion point for the 21-40 salaried audience: many cardholders equate "I paid before the due date" with "I paid it off," when paying only the Minimum Amount Due (MAD) is a different thing entirely.
- Mechanism-led topic fits the Trap/Warning and Behaviour Carousel formats in the mastermind doc, and rests on a stable, well-documented RBI rule rather than a fragile statistic, so it stays accurate over time.
- Evergreen: not tied to a single company or a fast-moving market event, so the core mechanism will still be true next year, reducing the need for frequent re-verification.

## Research notes

### 1. Interest-free period is conditional, not guaranteed
If a cardholder does not clear the **previous month's bill in full**, the interest-free period on new transactions is suspended. Interest is then charged from the **transaction date**, not from the payment due date. This is disclosed in card Most Important Terms and Conditions (MITC) documents per RBI direction. [VERIFY: exact clause reference in 2025 consolidated Master Direction]

### 2. Minimum Amount Due (MAD) formula
Generally the higher of:
- 100% of interest, fees and taxes billed that cycle, or
- Roughly 5% of the total outstanding
plus any past-due amount and any EMI instalments already due on the card.
This is an industry-standard formula disclosed by issuers (SBI, HDFC examples reviewed); the exact percentage can vary by issuer and product, so on-slide copy uses "about 5%" rather than a fixed universal number. [VERIFY: current MAD percentage per specific issuer before naming any bank]

### 3. Paying only the minimum still means interest on the FULL outstanding, not just the unpaid part
Since a rule effective 1 December 2022, if a cardholder pays only the MAD, finance charges apply to the **entire outstanding balance** (not merely the unpaid remainder), calculated from the original transaction date. This is the central, counter-intuitive mechanism for this carousel. [VERIFY: confirm this remains current RBI-mandated practice in 2026 against the primary Master Direction text before publishing]

### 4. RBI's ban on negative amortization
RBI's Master Directions state the Minimum Amount Due must be structured so that it does not result in "negative amortization" — meaning unpaid interest should not simply pile up and compound in a way that traps the balance from ever shrinking. This is a consumer-protection clause worth naming as the "recognised" nature of the trap, i.e., it is well known by the regulator. [VERIFY: exact regulatory language]

### 5. Late payment / credit bureau reporting grace
Card issuers may report an account as "past due" to Credit Information Companies (CICs) or levy late-payment penal charges only once the account has been "past due" for more than 3 days. This explains why a cardholder who pays MAD by the due date can look completely fine to their credit score and to the bank's records, while still paying interest on the full balance. This supports the "recognition" beat (feels fine, isn't). [VERIFY: exact number of days and scope in current 2025/2026 Master Direction]

### 6. Interest rate range
Typical credit card finance charges quoted in the sources reviewed run up to roughly 3% to 3.75% per month (annualised roughly 36% to 45%). This range varies significantly by issuer and card product and is not a fixed regulatory rate — RBI does not cap credit card interest rates; issuers set their own rates within board-approved policies. On-slide copy uses this only as an illustrative "up to" range, not attributed to any single bank. [VERIFY: current published rate card for any bank named; none are named in this draft]

## Claims included in the carousel (on-slide)

- Paying the bill "on time" is not the same as paying it "in full."
- If the previous bill was not paid in full, the interest-free period is suspended and interest is charged from the transaction date.
- Paying only the Minimum Amount Due typically means interest is charged on the entire outstanding balance, not just the part left unpaid.
- Minimum Amount Due is usually a small fraction of the bill (illustrated as "about 5%"), designed to keep the account looking current, not to bring the balance down quickly.
- Credit card interest, when it applies, is charged monthly and compounds if left unpaid across cycles, so the illustrative "up to 3-3.75% a month" range is used with a "check your card's MITC" qualifier, not stated as universal.
- The account can still look "current" or "safe" while this interest builds, because a few days of grace exist before an account is marked past due to a credit bureau.

No specific bank, card product, or exact APR is named. No specific SEBI/market data is used since this is a personal-finance-behaviour topic, not a markets topic.
