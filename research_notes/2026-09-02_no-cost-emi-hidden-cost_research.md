# Research Notes: No-Cost EMI Hidden Cost

## Topic selected

"No-cost EMI" / "zero-cost EMI" schemes on phones, appliances and other
consumer purchases, and why the "no cost" framing is misleading even
though it is not illegal to advertise.

Central idea: **The cost of a "no-cost EMI" purchase does not disappear.
It moves — usually out of a discount you would otherwise have gotten,
and sometimes into a fee.**

## Why it was selected

- Matches carousel_topic_bank.md priority item #1 ("Why zero-cost EMI is
  not always a free decision").
- No topic has been drafted or published from this repository before
  (published_log.csv and carousel_topic_bank.md "Published" section are

- Fits priority tier 3 (a common and expensive money mistake) and tier 5
  (a practical, evergreen finance-behaviour insight). "No-cost EMI"
  banners are a near-daily checkout experience for the 21-40 salaried
  and first-time-borrower audience this page targets (phones,
  electronics, furniture, e-commerce checkout).
- The mechanism has a real regulatory anchor (RBI's 2013 circular
  calling zero-percent interest "non-existent"), which supports the
  page's "explain the mechanism, not just the headline" voice without
  needing fresh breaking news.
- One-idea test passes: "The 'no-cost' label on an EMI purchase does not
  mean the cost is gone. It means the cost has been moved or renamed."

## Primary/authoritative sources consulted

1. RBI circular RBI/2013-14/292, DBS.CO.PPD No. 3578/11.01.005/2013-14,
   dated 17 September 2013 ("Loading of Credit Card Cash and Special
   Purchase / Zero % EMI Schemes" — commonly referred to as the RBI
   circular disallowing "zero percent interest" credit-card EMI

   and near-identical language: interest on zero-percent EMI schemes is

2. General, stable mechanism facts (not sourced to a single article,
   general well-established market practice in India, still current in
   2026):
   - Merchants typically offer a cash/instant discount to full up-front
     payers. On a "no-cost EMI" purchase this discount is commonly
     withdrawn, and the value of the withdrawn discount is what funds
     the "waived" interest.
   - Card-issuing banks/NBFCs commonly levy a processing fee on EMI
     conversion; this fee is separate from the advertised "0% interest"
     line and is where residual interest cost can be recovered.
   - GST is applicable on processing/conversion fees charged by
     lenders, because these are financial service fees. (Exact GST
     rate is not stated on-slide in this carousel because slab/fee
     numbers vary and can change — flagged as [VERIFY] below and
     excluded from slide copy.)

## Claims included in the carousel (on-slide)

- "No-cost EMI" is a marketing label, not a description of the true
  cost. Framed generically, no invented statistics.
- The cash discount you would get for paying up front is commonly
  removed when you choose EMI. (General, well-established retail
  practice — no specific percentage claimed on any slide.)
- A processing fee, GST on that fee where applicable, or a marked-up
  product price is where the "waived" interest usually shows up.
  (Framed with "often" / "commonly," not as a universal rule.)
- RBI has previously taken the position that zero-percent interest as a
  standalone concept does not exist in these schemes, and required
  transparent, uniform disclosure of interest and processing charges.
  (Cited as a 2013 regulatory position with source, not presented as a
  brand-new 2026 rule.)
- The math worth doing before saying yes to EMI: compare the total
  cash-payment price (after discount) to the total EMI payment
  (including any processing fee), not just the advertised monthly
  number.
