## Research notes

- **The core mechanism**: RBI's long-standing position (2013 circular) is
  that a genuinely interest-free loan does not exist commercially. When a
  bank or NBFC advertises "no-cost EMI," the interest cost has not been
  removed, it has been moved. It typically shows up as one or both of:
  1. A forgone cash/instant discount — the same product is often cheaper if
     paid in full upfront; choosing EMI usually means giving up that
     discount, and that foregone discount functions as the interest.
  2. A processing fee charged by the bank/NBFC to originate the EMI, which
     may attract GST on top.
- **Credit limit impact**: when a no-cost EMI is booked on a credit card,
  the full purchase value is blocked against the card's credit limit for
  the entire tenure (not just the current instalment). This raises reported
  credit utilisation for that period, which can affect a credit score even
  though the buyer is paying on time.
- **Credit bureau reporting**: no-cost EMI is a credit facility and is
  typically reported to bureaus. Timely payment can help a credit history;
  missed payments hurt it, exactly like a normal loan.
- **Numeric example used on Slide 5**: the ₹50,000 phone on a ₹1,00,000
  limit example is used in the carousel as an illustrative, clearly
  hypothetical arithmetic example (round numbers, not attributed to any
  real lender, cardholder or transaction), not a reported statistic. No
  source attaches a real-world figure to a specific bank, so it is framed
  on-slide as an illustration rather than a claim about any actual card
  or issuer.
