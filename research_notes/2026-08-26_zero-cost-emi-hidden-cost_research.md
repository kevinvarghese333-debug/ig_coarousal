# Research Notes: Zero-Cost EMI Is Not Actually Zero

## Topic Selected

Why "no cost EMI" / "zero cost EMI" checkout offers are not actually free, and what the real cost mechanism is (forgone cash discount, processing fee, GST on that fee).

## Why It Was Selected

- Fits priority topic #1 in `carousel_topic_bank.md` ("Why zero-cost EMI is not always a free decision"), unused, no 30-day repeat conflict since this is the first run in the log
- Common and expensive money mistake (priority tier 3), extremely relatable to the 21-40 salaried/first-time-borrower audience who buy phones and appliances on EMI
- Strong save/share potential: gives a concrete 3-step check people can use at their next checkout, and a fact worth forwarding to family
- One sharp central idea, passes the one-idea test: "The no-cost EMI button relabels the cost of borrowing, it does not remove it."

## Research Notes

The consistent, cross-source story across every source found:

1. When a bank/NBFC offers "no cost EMI," the buyer does not pay a separately labelled interest line at checkout.
2. That does not mean the cost of financing vanished. Two things commonly happen instead, in some combination depending on the seller and bank:
   - The seller withholds a cash/instant discount that would otherwise have been available for paying upfront, effectively funding the "waived" interest.
   - The bank charges a processing fee (commonly reported in the ₹99 to ₹499 range depending on bank and ticket size), and GST is charged on that fee.
3. RBI's 2013 position (per secondary reporting, not yet confirmed against the primary circular) was that banks should not market these schemes as "zero percent" because the cost is simply moved into fees, and that processing fee and rate of interest should be applied uniformly regardless of how the loan was sourced.
4. Reward points are commonly reported as not accruing on EMI-converted transactions, though this varies by issuer and was not used as an on-slide claim because it is not uniformly true across all cards.

## Claims Included in the Carousel

- "No cost EMI" schemes typically fund the "waived" interest through a forgone upfront/cash discount and/or a processing fee. Presented as a general, widely-reported pattern, phrased with "may," "can," and "often" rather than as a universal rule about every bank or seller.
- RBI has previously said the concept of zero percent interest is not real and that such costs are commonly folded into fees. Presented as historical regulatory context (2013), phrased cautiously, with the exact circular number and quote marked [VERIFY] in the draft since the primary source could not be reached this run.
- Processing fees on EMI conversions commonly attract GST. Presented generally, without stating a specific rate on-slide, since the current rate should be checked before publishing.
- The ₹40,000 phone / ₹38,000 upfront / ₹499 fee example on slide 5 is an invented, illustrative example built to demonstrate the mechanism with round numbers. It is explicitly labelled on-slide as "AN ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE, NOT A SPECIFIC PRODUCT" and is not attributed to any real bank, retailer or product.

## Claims Removed Because They Were Not Adequately Verified

- A specific current GST percentage was considered for on-slide use but removed from visible copy. GST rates and applicability can change and this run could not confirm the current rate against a primary CBIC/GST source. The draft's Slide 5 receipt uses the rounded, clearly illustrative figure "about ₹590" for combined fee and GST rather than stating a specific rate as fact.
- A specific bank-by-bank processing fee figure (e.g. "HDFC charges ₹299") was considered and removed. Fees vary by bank, card and ticket size and were not verified against any bank's current, official fee schedule.
- Reward-point forfeiture on EMI transactions was considered as a slide point and removed because it is issuer-dependent and not universal.
- Any claim implying RBI has "banned" no-cost EMI outright was avoided. Reporting is mixed on whether this was a ban versus a disclosure/uniformity requirement, so the draft uses the more cautious framing that RBI called the "zero percent" framing misleading and required uniform fees/rates, rather than asserting a blanket ban.
