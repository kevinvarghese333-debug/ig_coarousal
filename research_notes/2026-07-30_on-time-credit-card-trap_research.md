# Research Notes: Why paying your credit card bill "on time" can still get you into money trouble

## Topic selected

Why clearing only the minimum amount due, by the due date, still leaves a
cardholder paying steep interest, and why RBI's new 3-day grace period
(effective April 1, 2027) does not change that.

## Research notes

The mechanism, in plain terms:

- A credit card bill shows two numbers prominently: "Total Amount Due" and
  "Minimum Amount Due." Many cardholders pay the minimum amount due by the
  due date and consider themselves to have paid "on time."
- Paying only the minimum due does stop a late fee and, once RBI's April
  2027 amendment takes effect, will stop an account being reported as
  "past due" to credit bureaus during the first 3 days after the due date.
- Neither of those protections touches interest. If a cardholder does not
  clear the full statement balance, the interest-free period is forfeited,
  and interest is charged on the outstanding balance until it is paid off,
  commonly calculated back to the transaction date depending on the
  issuer's terms.
- The RBI amendment matters for this carousel specifically because it
  creates a natural, current news hook, and because the "grace period"
  framing in consumer reporting on the amendment can easily be
  misread by a reader as "I now have more breathing room on interest too."
  The carousel's central tension is correcting that specific
  misunderstanding: the grace period is about fees and credit reporting,
  not about interest.
