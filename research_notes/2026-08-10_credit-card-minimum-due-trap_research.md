# Research Notes: The Minimum Due Trap

## Topic selected

Why people who pay their credit card bill "on time" can still end up in
money trouble: paying only the minimum amount due, before the due date,
still cancels the interest-free period and triggers high interest from
the original transaction date.

  as the model example of a sharp central idea in
  `whenkevintalks_carousel_design_mastermind.md`, confirming it fits the

## Primary sources consulted

1. **Reserve Bank of India, Master Direction – Credit Card and Debit
   Card – Issuance and Conduct Directions, 2022** (and FAQ document).
   - Source: https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/NotificationUser.aspx?Id=12342&Mode=0
   - FAQ PDF: https://www.rbi.org.in/commonman/Upload/English/FAQs/PDFs/FAQMDCreditCardandDebitCard.pdf
   - Source date accessed: 2026-08-10
   - Relevant provisions: card issuers must structure the minimum amount
     due so there is no negative amortisation; unpaid charges/levies/
     taxes cannot be capitalised for compounding; issuers must disclose
     annualised percentage rates (APR) for situations including
     non-payment of minimum amount due and late payment.

2. **RBI guidance summarised via secondary reporting** (used to confirm
   the minimum-due formula and interest-free-period mechanics, since the
   full Master Direction PDF was accessed via search summary rather than
   full-text read in this session):
   - Minimum Payment Due = higher of (100% of interest + fees + taxes,
     OR 5% of total payment due) + higher of (past due amount, over-limit
     amount) + any EMI instalments due.
   - If the total amount due is not cleared by the due date, the
     interest-free period is lost and finance charges apply from the
     transaction date on the outstanding balance.
   - Cross-referenced across: Airtel RBI guidelines explainer
     (https://www.airtel.in/blog/credit-card/rbi-guidelines-for-credit-card-latest-updates/),
     Finowings RBI credit card rules explainer
     (https://www.finowings.com/Credit-Cards/rbi-credit-card-rules),
     BankBazaar RBI rules page
     (https://www.bankbazaar.com/credit-card/rbi-rules-on-credit-card.html).
   - Source dates: pages describe 2025-26 guidance, accessed 2026-08-10.
   - Status: directionally consistent across all sources and consistent
     with the primary RBI Master Direction protections (no negative
     amortisation, APR disclosure). The exact 5% minimum-due formula
     wording is common industry practice reported consistently but is
     marked [VERIFY] below since it was not read directly from the
     primary PDF text in this session.

3. **Typical credit card interest rates in India**:
   - Range cited: 2.5% to 3.75% per month, i.e. roughly 30% to 45-48%
     annualised.
   - Sources: Bajaj Finserv Markets
     (https://www.bajajfinservmarkets.in/credit-card/credit-card-interest-rates),
     Forbes Advisor India
     (https://www.forbes.com/advisor/in/credit-card/credit-card-interest-rate/),
     Fincash (https://www.fincash.com/l/pf/credit-card-interest-rate).
   - Source dates: 2026 pages, accessed 2026-08-10.
   - Status: rates vary by issuer and cardholder profile. Used on-slide
     only as a range ("30 to 48% a year"), not a single fixed number, and
     framed as typical/market range rather than a guaranteed rate for any
     individual card.

4. **Credit utilisation ratio and CIBIL score**:
   - Widely reported industry guidance: keep credit utilisation below
     roughly 30% of the total available limit; utilisation is one of the
     factors credit bureaus weigh (commonly cited around 30% of the
     score, alongside payment history and credit age).
   - Sources: IIFL (https://www.iifl.com/blogs/credit-score/credit-utilisation-ratio-what-it-is-and-why-it-matters),
     Paytm blog (https://paytm.com/blog/credit-score/how-your-credit-utilization-ratio-directly-impacts-your-cibil-score/).
   - Source dates: accessed 2026-08-10.
   - Status: this is bureau/industry guidance and general practice, not
     an RBI regulation. On-slide copy uses "above 30%" as commonly cited
     guidance and avoids stating it as a hard rule or guaranteed score
     impact.

## Claims included in the carousel

- Paying only the minimum due, even before the due date, ends the
  interest-free period on the full statement balance (supported by RBI
  Master Direction protections + consistent secondary reporting).
- Interest is then charged from the original transaction date, not from
  the due date (consistent secondary reporting, aligned with RBI's
  no-negative-amortisation framing).
- Typical credit card interest in India runs roughly 30 to 48% a year,
  stated as a range, not a specific card's rate.
- Carrying a balance raises credit utilisation, and utilisation above
  roughly 30% is commonly cited as a factor that can pull a credit score
  down. Framed as general guidance ("can," "commonly cited"), not a
  universal rule for every bureau model or individual.
- New spends on a card with an open balance typically do not get a fresh
  interest-free grace period either, since the interest-free period
  applies only when the previous statement was paid in full.

## Claims removed because they were not adequately verified

- An exact single minimum-due percentage (e.g. "always 5%") was removed
  from on-slide copy. Card issuers vary in the exact formula and rate
  within RBI's structural rules, so the copy refers to "minimum due" as
  a small percentage without asserting one fixed universal number as
  regulation. The worked example on Slide 5 uses 5% explicitly labelled
  as an illustrative example figure, not a stated universal rate.
- A single fixed APR figure (e.g. "42% a year" as fact for all cards) was
  removed in favour of the sourced range "30 to 48% a year," and the
  worked-example month uses 42% explicitly framed as an illustrative
  example rate, not a claimed universal rate.
- No specific bank, card product, or company name is used anywhere in
  the carousel, since no single issuer's current rate was verified
  against a primary bank source.
