# Research Notes: Why Paying the Minimum Due On Time Still Costs You

## Topic selected

Why people who pay their credit-card minimum due on time still lose money to
interest, even though their credit score and account status stay clean.

## Why it was selected

- Matches priority topic bank item 2 ("Why people who pay their credit-card
  bill on time still get into money trouble"), unused in the prior 30 days
  (published_log.csv is empty; this is the first run).
- Universal Indian salaried-audience behaviour: minimum due payment is common
  and widely misunderstood as "safe."
- Strong hidden-mechanism angle (RBI rule change is genuinely counter to
  popular belief that "on time = no interest").
- High save/share potential: practical, protects the reader from a real cost,
  gives a decision rule.
- No company-specific or market-data risk, so verification surface is small
  and well-covered by a primary regulatory source.

## Primary sources consulted

1. **RBI, Master Direction - Credit Card and Debit Card - Issuance and
   Conduct Directions, 2022** (issued 21 April 2022, updated 07 March 2024).
   https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/BS_ViewMasDirections.aspx?id=12300
   - Confirms: if a cardholder does not clear the total amount due by the
     payment due date, the interest-free period is lost and interest is
     charged from the date of the transaction on the outstanding balance
     (adjusted for payments, refunds and reversals), not only on the unpaid
     portion.
   - Confirms: no negative amortisation - finance charges/taxes are not
     capitalised into the next statement's principal.
   - Confirms: late payment charges apply only to the overdue amount, not
     the full amount due (separate from the interest rule above), and an
     account is reported "past due" only if overdue by more than 3 days.
   - Rule on interest applying to the entire outstanding balance (not just
     the unpaid part) has applied since 1 December 2022.

2. Secondary confirmation of the same RBI rule and its practical effect:
   - Business Standard, "Why only paying the minimum due on credit card may
     not be a wise move" (12 Dec 2024).
     https://www.business-standard.com/amp/finance/personal-finance/why-only-paying-the-minimum-due-on-credit-card-may-not-be-a-wise-move-124121300923_1.html
   - EMI Calculator, "RBI Asks Banks to Revisit Minimum Amount Due Formula."
     https://emicalculator.net/rbi-asks-banks-to-revisit-minimum-amount-due-formula/

3. Effect of minimum-due payment on credit score/CIBIL:
   - MoneyView, "Does Paying Minimum Due Affect CIBIL and Credit Score"
     (accessed 25 Aug 2026).
     https://moneyview.in/cibil-score/does-paying-minimum-due-affect-cibil-score
   - BankBazaar, "How Making Minimum Payments Affects Your Credit Score."
     https://www.bankbazaar.com/cibil/does-making-minimum-payment-affects-credit-score.html
   - Consensus: paying at least the minimum due, on time, avoids a late-
     payment mark and keeps the account "in good standing" (no immediate
     score hit for the payment itself). However, carrying a high outstanding
     balance raises credit utilisation, which can hurt the score over time.
     This is an indirect, longer-term effect, not the same as the interest
     mechanism, so it is kept as a secondary caveat rather than the main
     on-slide claim.

4. Typical credit card interest rate range (for the "escalation" slide):
   - BankBazaar, "Credit Cards with Low Interest Rates in India 2026."
     https://www.bankbazaar.com/credit-card/low-interest-rate-credit-cards.html
   - HDFC Bank, "What is Credit Card Interest Rate" (2026).
     https://www.hdfc.bank.in/blogs/credit-cards/what-is-credit-card-interest-rate
   - ICICI Bank, "What Is Credit Card Interest Rate" (2026).
     https://www.icici.bank.in/personal-banking/blogs/card/credit-card/interest-rates
   - Range found across issuers: roughly 2.5% to 3.75% per month, commonly
     summarised as approximately 30% to 45% per year. Some premium cards
     price lower (e.g., HDFC Infinia from 2.5%/month); some cards price
     higher. Treated on-slide as an approximate, issuer-varying range, not
     a single fixed number, with a "check your card's terms" caveat.

## Claims included in the carousel

- Paying the minimum due by the due date avoids a late-payment mark and
  keeps the account in "good standing." [Sourced: MoneyView, BankBazaar]
- Paying only the minimum due does not stop interest. Interest is charged on
  the entire outstanding balance, calculated from the date of each
  transaction, not from the missed due date and not only on the unpaid
  part. [Sourced: RBI Master Direction, 2022/2024]
- This interest-from-transaction-date rule applies once the full amount due
  is not cleared, regardless of whether the minimum due was paid on time.
  [Sourced: RBI Master Direction]
- Credit card interest commonly runs in the approximate range of 30% to 45%
  a year, varying by card and issuer. [Sourced: BankBazaar, HDFC, ICICI;
  phrased as an approximate range, not a single number]
- High outstanding balances (linked to minimum-due-only payment) raise
  credit utilisation, which can affect the score over time even though the
  minimum-due payment itself does not. [Sourced: MoneyView, BankBazaar]

## Claims removed because they were not adequately verified

- Any specific bank's exact interest rate (varies by card, relationship,
  and changes frequently) - removed from on-slide copy, kept only as a
  general range with a "check your card's current terms" caveat.
- Any claim about a specific rupee amount of interest a real cardholder
  paid - not used. The ₹40,000 bill / ₹2,000 minimum due / ₹38,000
  outstanding figures on Slide 5 are a clearly labelled hypothetical
  illustration used to explain the mechanism, not a sourced statistic.
- Any claim tying this mechanism to a specific bank or NBFC by name -
  removed to avoid an unverified, singled-out claim against one issuer. The
  rule is a regulatory (RBI) requirement that applies system-wide.
- Grace-period length ("18 to 50 days") - mentioned only as background in
  this research file; not used on-slide because it varies too much by card
  to state as a single figure.
