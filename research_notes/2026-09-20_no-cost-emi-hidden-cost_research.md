# Research Notes: No-Cost EMI Hidden Cost

## Topic selected

Why "no-cost EMI" is not actually free credit, and how the cost is moved
rather than removed.

## Why this topic was selected

- Pulled from `carousel_topic_bank.md` priority list, item 1: "Why zero-cost
  EMI is not always a free decision."
- No topics have been published in the last 30 days (published_log.csv is
  empty; this is the first production run), so no repeat-topic conflict.
- Matches priority order rule 3 (a common and expensive money mistake) and
  rule 2 (a common audience confusion): "no cost EMI" is one of the most
  frequently used checkout options for salaried 21 to 40 year olds buying
  phones, appliances and electronics in India, and the phrase "no cost" is
  widely misread as "interest free."
- Behaviour-carousel type, evergreen, not tied to a fast-moving market
  event, which keeps the fact-risk low while still requiring real
  verification of the regulatory and tax mechanics.

## Research notes

- In September 2013, RBI told banks that "zero per cent interest" on
  consumer-durable and credit-card EMI schemes is not a real product: the
  interest cost still exists, it is simply redistributed, often folded into
  processing charges or into the price of the product. RBI's stated
  position, as reported: fair practice requires processing charges and
  interest to be applied uniformly, and the concept of zero per cent
  interest is "non-existent."
- The mechanism reported consistently across consumer-finance explainers
  (Paisabazaar, BankBazaar, CRIF High Mark): sellers frequently offer a
  cash discount to customers who pay the full price upfront. When a buyer
  chooses "no cost EMI" instead, that cash discount is typically withdrawn.
  The value of the withdrawn discount is what funds the interest the
  lender (bank or NBFC) still charges on the EMI. The customer pays close
  to the same total amount either way; the difference is who calls it what.
- Processing fees: most sources agree a processing fee can still apply on a
  no-cost EMI conversion, though some issuers waive it during promotions.
  This varies by issuer, card and offer, so the carousel uses hedged
  language ("can," "may," "check the current terms") rather than a
  universal claim.
- GST: multiple sources (ClearTax, BankBazaar, Busy) confirm GST is charged
  at 18% on the processing/conversion fee for credit-card EMI, consistent
  with the standard GST rate on financial service fees. GST is not charged
  on the underlying principal. This is treated as a well-corroborated,
  low-volatility fact (standard GST slab on financial services), but is
  still marked [VERIFY] below because the rate is set by tax policy and can
  change.

## Claims included in the carousel

1. No-cost EMI schemes are commonly built around withdrawing a cash/upfront
   discount rather than genuinely waiving interest. (Slide 4, Slide 7)
   — well corroborated across five independent consumer-finance sources.
2. A processing fee can still apply on a no-cost EMI conversion, depending
   on the issuer, and GST can apply on that fee. (Slide 6, Slide 8)
   — corroborated, phrased with hedging words per source rules.
3. RBI's long-standing position (from 2013 onward, repeatedly referenced by
   finance publishers) is that "zero percent interest" is not a real
   product category and that costs must be applied uniformly. (Slide 4
   context; referenced generally, not quoted or cited to a specific
   clause on-slide.)
