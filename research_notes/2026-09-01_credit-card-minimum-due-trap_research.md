# Research Notes: The Minimum Due Trap

## Topic selected

Why paying only the "minimum amount due" on a credit card, month after month, quietly becomes one of the most expensive habits in personal finance, even for people who never technically default.

## Primary sources consulted

1. **RBI, Master Direction – Credit Card and Debit Card – Issuance and Conduct Directions, 2022** (dated April 21, 2022, effective July 1, 2022). Requires that terms for payment of credit card dues, including the minimum amount due, be structured so there is **no negative amortisation** (i.e., the minimum due must be enough to cover at least the interest and a portion of principal, so the balance does not grow even while "minimum due" is paid every month).
   - Source: RBI Master Direction, summarised via KSandK Advisors newsletter and TaxGuru amendment commentary (RBI's own rbi.org.in master direction page could not be fetched directly inside this environment; secondary legal/regulatory summaries corroborate the same provision from the same document, dated April 21, 2022 / effective July 1, 2022).
   - Retrieved: 2026-09-01.

2. **RBI amendment to the above Master Direction, effective March 7, 2024.** Strengthened disclosure requirements around minimum-payment structures and added stricter rules for how issuers must communicate the consequences of paying only the minimum due.
   - Source: Bar & Bench, "Enhancing consumer protection and regulation: Amendments to Master Direction on Credit Card and Debit Card issue and conduct"; corroborated by ksandk.com and taxguru.in summaries.
   - Retrieved: 2026-09-01.

3. **Grace period / interest-free period mechanics.** Multiple independent Indian fintech and bank-adjacent explainers (BankBazaar, Axis Bank blog, Bajaj Finserv Markets, InvestingPro) consistently describe the same mechanism: the interest-free ("grace") period on a credit card, typically cited in the 20-to-50-day range depending on the card and billing cycle, applies only when the previous statement was paid **in full**. If any amount is carried forward, including via the minimum-due payment, the grace period is lost and new transactions can start accruing interest from the date of purchase, not from a future due date.
   - Sources: BankBazaar "Interest Free Grace Period On Your Credit Card"; Axis Bank "What Is Grace Period for Credit Card Payment"; InvestingPro "Credit Card Billing Cycle Explained: Due Dates, Interest & Minimum Payment Trap."
   - Retrieved: 2026-09-01.
   - Status: **[VERIFY]** — the exact number of grace-period days varies by issuer and card product. Used only as a range ("often between 20 and 50 days") with "check your card's terms" framing; no single issuer's number is presented as universal.

4. **Interest is charged on the outstanding amount, not a fixed flat fee.** RBI's Master Direction requires interest to be levied only on the outstanding amount, net of payments and reversed transactions, for the relevant period. Multiple explainer sources also note that once the grace period is lost, interest is commonly computed from the transaction date rather than the statement or due date.
   - Status: **[VERIFY]** for the exact day-count convention per issuer; the general "interest resets to the outstanding balance and applies from the transaction date once the grace period is lost" framing is corroborated across sources but the specific compounding method can vary by bank. On-slide copy avoids stating a specific annualised interest rate for this reason.

## Claims included in the carousel (verified or safely framed)

- Paying only the minimum due keeps the account technically "not overdue," so it does not show up as a default or directly damage the credit score the way a missed payment would.
- RBI requires the minimum-due formula to avoid negative amortisation, meaning by regulation the minimum due must cover more than pure principal reduction, i.e. it must absorb interest and fees first, which is why balances can stay stubbornly high even with regular minimum payments.
- Carrying any unpaid balance forward, including through minimum-due payments, causes most Indian card issuers to withdraw the interest-free grace period on new transactions, not only on the unpaid amount.
- No specific interest rate, fee amount, or number of grace-period days is stated as a fixed universal figure on the slides. Slide copy uses "many cards," "often," and "check your card's terms" language per the routine's safety rules.

## Claims removed or deliberately excluded from on-slide copy

- Removed: any specific annualised interest rate (commonly cited ranges vary widely by issuer, roughly high-20s to mid-40s percent, and change with RBI/issuer policy) — too fragile to state as fact on a slide meant to stay accurate over time.
- Removed: an exact number of interest-free/grace-period days — varies by card and billing cycle; used only as a broad "20 to 50 days" range in the research file, not stated as a fixed number on any slide.
- Removed: any claim naming a specific bank, NBFC, or card product — the mechanism is described as a regulatory/industry-wide pattern (with "many issuers" / "most cards" language), not attributed to any single named lender, avoiding both misrepresentation and personalised-advice risk.
- No investment, credit, or borrowing advice is given to an individual; slide 8's "decision rule" is a general mental model, not a personal recommendation.
