# No Cost EMI Already Has a Cost

## Status

Production complete. Review before Instagram publishing.

## Strategic Premise

- Topic: No Cost EMI at checkout is not genuinely free financing.
- Central idea: "No Cost EMI" is a seller discount rebuilt into the loan as interest, and the "free" part is usually the first thing you lose if you cancel, return, or close the EMI early.
- Primary audience: Indian salaried professionals and first-time borrowers, 21 to 40, buying phones, appliances or gadgets on EMI, especially around festive-sale season.
- Audience tension: they feel smart for choosing the "zero interest" option and rarely check what the cash price actually was.
- Primary engagement goal: saves and shares (this is a decision-framework carousel people forward before their next purchase).
- Why people may save, share or comment: it gives a concrete 3-question checklist to use before tapping "No Cost EMI," it reframes a familiar checkout moment they have all done without thinking, and it ends with a direct, answerable question.

## Five Cover Hooks

1. Curiosity hook: "That 'No Cost EMI' button already has a cost. You just have not paid it yet."
2. Contrarian hook: "Zero percent interest does not legally exist in India. So where did it go?"
3. Relatable-pain hook: "You tapped 'No Cost EMI' without checking the price without it."
4. Hidden-business-mechanism hook: "The store is not being generous. It moved the discount somewhere you cannot see."
5. Warning-or-risk hook: "Cancel a 'No Cost EMI' order, and the free part disappears first."

**Selected hook:** No Cost EMI already has a cost. The receipt just has not shown it yet.

**Why it wins:** It names a checkout action almost every reader has taken, states a specific claim rather than a broad topic, and creates an immediate question (where is the cost?) that only the swipe can answer.

## Caption

Every festive sale season, checkout asks the same question: pay full price today, or split it with "No Cost EMI," interest showing zero. The second option feels like a favour. It usually is not.

Zero percent interest is not really a lending product in India. What looks free is typically a seller's discount, moved upfront and repackaged as "no" interest, while a processing fee can still sit on the invoice, and GST can apply to that fee and the interest portion.

The expensive moment is rarely day one. It tends to be the day you return the product, close the EMI early, or miss a due date, because that is usually when the "free" part quietly disappears from your refund or your bill.

None of this makes EMI a bad tool. It means "No Cost" is doing a lot of marketing work for a normal pricing decision.

Before your next "No Cost EMI" tap: have you ever actually checked what the cash price was?
