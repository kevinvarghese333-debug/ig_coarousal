# No Cost EMI Is Not a Free Decision

## Status

Draft awaiting Kevin's fact-check and approval

## Strategic Premise

- Topic: Why "No Cost EMI" is not actually interest-free
- Central idea: The bank never lends for free. When an EMI plan shows zero interest, the cost has not disappeared, it has moved into a forfeited cash discount, a processing fee, or GST on the interest baked into the price.
- Primary audience: Salaried 21 to 40 year old Indians buying phones, appliances or gadgets on card EMI, who treat "no cost" as literally free credit
- Audience tension: They feel smart for "avoiding interest," when the checkout screen is actually designed to make them stop comparing prices
- Primary engagement goal: Saves (decision rule people screenshot before their next big purchase) and comments (people sharing what they paid extra without noticing)
- Why people may save, share or comment: It reframes a purchase decision almost everyone in this audience has made recently, gives them a concrete rule to check before the next one, and hands them a fact worth sending to a family WhatsApp group before someone buys a fridge on "no cost" EMI

## Five Cover Hooks

1. Curiosity hook: "No cost EMI. Zero interest. So where does that money actually go?"
2. Contrarian hook: "No cost EMI is not interest-free. It is interest you can't see."
3. Relatable-pain hook: "You chose 'No Cost EMI' to save money. You may have paid more."
4. Hidden-business-mechanism hook: "The bank never lends for free. Someone always pays the interest."
5. Warning-or-risk hook: "That 'No Cost EMI' badge at checkout is doing exactly what it was designed to do."

**Selected hook:** "You chose 'No Cost EMI' to save money. You may have paid more."

**Why it wins:** It puts the viewer inside their own recent decision instead of explaining a concept at them, which creates the "wait, did I actually pay more?" reaction that drives a stop and a swipe.

## Caption

Zero-cost EMI feels like a loophole. Same phone, same price, no interest line on your statement. It is not a loophole. It is a redesign of where the cost sits.

The bank does not lend for free. When you see 0% interest, someone paid that interest already, usually the merchant, funded by the cash discount you did not get. Add a processing fee some banks still charge, and GST on the interest that technically still exists, and "no cost" starts looking more like "no visible cost."

None of this means EMI is bad. Spreading a genuine, planned purchase over a few months is a normal tool. The problem is when "no cost" becomes the reason you buy something you would not have bought in cash. The label is doing the persuading, not your judgement.

Before your next "No Cost EMI" checkout screen, check the cash price first. Compare it to the EMI price. The gap tells you the real cost of the credit, whatever the checkout screen calls it.
