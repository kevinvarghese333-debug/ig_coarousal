# Zero Percent Interest Does Not Exist

## Status

Production complete. Review before Instagram publishing.

## Strategic Premise

- Topic: "No Cost EMI" and where its interest cost actually goes.
- Central idea: No-cost EMI removes the word interest from the checkout screen. It does not remove the cost. That cost usually shows up as a lost cash discount or a higher listed price.
- Primary audience: Salaried professionals and first-time borrowers aged 21 to 40 who use EMI at checkout for phones, appliances and electronics.
- Audience tension: They feel smart for "avoiding interest" while quietly giving up a discount or restraint they would otherwise have kept.
- Primary engagement goal: Saves and shares. This is a decision rule people want to remember at their next checkout, and a fact worth sending to a friend before a festive-sale purchase.
- Why people may save, share or comment: It gives a concrete, one-line test to run before any "No Cost EMI" purchase, it cites a real RBI position that most people have never heard, and it invites a comment about a specific EMI decision.

## Five Cover Hooks

1. Curiosity hook: "The receipt says zero. The math disagrees."
2. Contrarian hook: "No Cost EMI is not free. It is renamed."
3. Relatable-pain hook: "You skipped the discount to feel like you saved money."
4. Hidden-business-mechanism hook: "Zero percent interest does not exist. RBI said so."
5. Warning-or-risk hook: "That 'No Cost EMI' button hides a discount you never asked about."

**Selected hook:** "Zero percent interest does not exist." (RBI, 2013)

**Why it wins:** It borrows RBI's own blunt language, so it reads as a fact revealed rather than an opinion argued, which earns the swipe instantly.

## Caption

"No Cost EMI" sounds like the bank did you a favour. In 2013, RBI told banks plainly that zero percent interest does not exist, and that the "waived" interest on these schemes was usually recovered from the customer another way, often through the processing fee instead of a visible interest line.

The label has since changed from "Zero Percent EMI" to "No Cost EMI," but the underlying arithmetic has not moved. Most of the time, choosing EMI over cash means giving up a discount you would have received for paying upfront. The store's price does not change. Your price does.

This is not a case against EMI. Sometimes spreading a payment is the right call for your cash flow. It is a case against believing "no cost" means no cost. Before your next big-ticket purchase, ask for the cash price and compare it yourself.

Have you ever checked the cash price against the EMI price on the same purchase, and were the numbers actually different?
