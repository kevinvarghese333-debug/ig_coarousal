# No-Cost EMI: Who Pays the Interest?

## Status

Production complete. Review before Instagram publishing.

## Strategic Premise

- Topic: Zero-cost / no-cost EMI on consumer purchases
- Central idea: A "no-cost" EMI is rarely free; the interest is moved into a lost discount, the price, or fees, and the cash-price versus EMI-total gap shows it.
- Primary audience: Indian salaried viewers aged 21 to 40 who buy phones and appliances on EMI
- Audience tension: The EMI looks painless, but nobody checks what the cash price would have been.
- Primary engagement goal: Saves (a checkout rule) and comments (personal checkout experiences)
- Why people may save, share or comment: It gives a three-step check usable at the next purchase, and the question invites real experiences.

## Five Cover Hooks

1. Curiosity hook: "No-cost" EMI. So who pays the interest?
2. Contrarian hook: There is no such thing as a free EMI.
3. Relatable-pain hook: You bought the phone on "zero interest". Did you check the cash price?
4. Hidden-business-mechanism hook: The seller is not giving you free credit. Here is where the interest goes.
5. Warning-or-risk hook: The EMI looked free. The price already had the interest in it.

**Selected hook:** "No-cost" EMI. So who pays the interest?

**Why it wins:** It names an everyday checkout phrase and asks a question the viewer cannot answer instantly, without alarm.

## Caption

See output/2026-10-15_no-cost-emi-who-pays-interest/caption.txt (identical text):

"No-cost EMI" sounds like the seller is doing you a favour. Usually, the interest has not gone anywhere. It has been moved.

Sometimes it sits in a cash discount you lose when you choose the EMI. Sometimes the price tag already has room for it. Sometimes it shows up as a processing fee or GST on the interest.

The example in this carousel uses made-up numbers: a phone at ₹57,000 in cash or 6 payments of ₹10,000. The ₹3,000 gap is the cost of the credit, and it works out to roughly 18% a year. Your real offer will differ, so check your own numbers.

RBI's credit card directions expect issuers to show the principal, the interest and any upfront discount before an EMI conversion. That breakdown is where the answer sits. Ask for it.

Sometimes paying for credit is a fair choice. The point is to know the number before you tap confirm.

Last time you bought something on no-cost EMI, did the cash price match the EMI total? What did you find?
