# Zero-Cost EMI Is Not a Free Decision

## Status

Production complete. Review before Instagram publishing.

## Strategic Premise

- Topic: "No-cost" / "zero-cost" EMI schemes on phones, laptops and appliances
- Central idea: Zero-cost EMI is not a free decision. The interest doesn't disappear, it moves from the interest line to a discount you quietly give up.
- Primary audience: Salaried Indian professionals aged 21-40 who buy phones, laptops or appliances on EMI, especially during festive sale season
- Audience tension: They feel smart clicking "No Cost EMI" and have never questioned where that cost actually went
- Primary engagement goal: Saves (this is a checkout-moment decision rule people will want to reopen before their next purchase) and shares (it corrects a widely believed myth)
- Why people may save, share or comment: It reframes a button they've personally tapped, gives a concrete way to check the real price next time, and invites a relatable admission ("I've fallen for this")

## Five Cover Hooks

1. Curiosity hook: "The EMI says zero cost. Your bill says otherwise."
2. Contrarian hook: "Zero-cost EMI is not a free decision."
3. Relatable-pain hook: "You chose EMI to save money. You paid more."
4. Hidden-business-mechanism hook: "Someone is paying your 'free' EMI interest. It is not the bank."
5. Warning-or-risk hook: "That 'no cost' button hides a cost you never see."

**Selected hook:** Zero-cost EMI is not a free decision.

**Why it wins:** It corrects the exact phrase the viewer has tapped at checkout, so it creates instant recognition and mild tension without needing a setup line.

## Caption

You have tapped "No Cost EMI" at checkout. It feels like a free upgrade: same price, spread across months, no extra charge. Except the lender is not lending for free. Someone is still paying the interest, usually the seller, and that cost tends to show up somewhere else in the price you are quoted, often as a discount you quietly do not get.

None of this makes EMI wrong. Spreading a large purchase can be a sensible way to manage cash flow. The problem is treating "no cost" as proof that nothing changed, when the honest comparison is the total you pay across every month against the price a cash buyer gets for the same product.

Before your next "No Cost EMI" tap, ask for the cash price on the same item. If the gap is small, the label was honest. If it is not, you already have your answer.

What is the most misleading checkout phrase you have ever seen?
