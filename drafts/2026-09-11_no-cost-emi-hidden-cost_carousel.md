# No-Cost EMI Is Not Free

## Status

Production complete. Review before Instagram publishing.

## Strategic Premise

- Topic: Why "zero-cost" / "no-cost" EMI is not actually free.
- Central idea: No-cost EMI does not delete the cost of borrowing. It moves the cost into a forfeited discount, a processing fee, GST, or looser spending behaviour.
- Primary audience: Salaried Indian professionals, 21 to 40, who buy phones, appliances or gadgets on EMI and feel smart for avoiding interest.
- Audience tension: People believe "0% EMI" means the purchase is free of extra cost, so they choose it automatically without checking what they are giving up.
- Primary engagement goal: Saves (a decision checklist) and comments (a real judgement question).
- Why people may save, share or comment: It reframes a purchase decision they make often, gives them a concrete checklist to use next time, and is the kind of "send this to someone about to buy a phone on EMI" content.

## Five Cover Hooks

1. Curiosity hook: "Zero cost. Zero is doing a lot of work in that sentence."
2. Contrarian hook: "No-cost EMI is not free. It just hides where the cost went."
3. Relatable-pain hook: "You picked 0% EMI to save money. The store may have quietly kept a discount instead."
4. Hidden-business-mechanism hook: "Someone is paying for your 'no-cost' EMI. It is not the bank."
5. Warning-or-risk hook: "RBI has a name for zero percent interest. Non-existent."

**Selected hook:** No-cost EMI is not free. It just hides where the cost went.

**Why it wins:** It states the contrarian claim in plain language, uses a familiar phrase the reader has tapped on a checkout screen, and creates an open loop ("hides where") that only the swipe can resolve.

## Caption

You tap "0% EMI" because it feels like the safer button. No interest, no cost, no downside. Except a bank never lends for free. When there is truly no interest on paper, the cost has usually moved somewhere else: a cash discount you did not get, a processing fee, GST on that fee, or simply the extra confidence EMI gives you to buy something you would have paused on in cash.

None of this means EMI is bad. It means "no cost" is a marketing phrase, not a financial fact, and RBI has been saying so since 2013. The fix is not to avoid EMI. It is to ask for the full cash price before you decide, so you are comparing two real numbers instead of one real number and one flattering one.

This is not a call to avoid EMI or a specific lender. It is a habit: ask the question before you tap the button.

What is one purchase where you later realised the "no cost" option was not actually the cheaper one?

- Image or screenshot requirements: None. No external screenshots, no fake app or bank interfaces. All visuals are code-rendered typographic and diagrammatic elements.
- Chart and data guidance: One simple three-node money-flow diagram (slide 4). One receipt/ledger motif that accumulates line items across slides 2, 4, 5, 6 and 8, then closes the loop on slide 9. No pie charts. Every number is either clearly illustrative or attributed.
- Mobile-readability notes: Headline sizes range 74 to 112 px depending on line length; body copy 34 to 44 px; fine print 26 to 30 px used sparingly and never carrying the main idea. All critical text kept inside a safe margin of at least 90 px from every edge.
- Accessibility notes: Off-white text on navy background exceeds standard contrast requirements. Gold accent used only for secondary emphasis, never as the sole carrier of meaning on its own. No text is placed over busy visual areas.
- Animation notes: Static carousel by default, per the mastermind guidance that static carousels can perform extremely well. No motion elements required for comprehension in this carousel.
