# Your Bill Says Paid. Your Interest Doesn't Agree.

## Status

Production complete. Review before Instagram publishing.

## Strategic Premise

- Topic: Paying only the Minimum Amount Due on a credit card, while believing the bill is handled.
- Central idea: You can pay your credit card bill "on time" every month and still lose money quietly, because paying only the minimum due does not stop interest from applying to your full outstanding balance, often from the date you spent it.
- Primary audience: Salaried Indian professionals, 21 to 40, who use a credit card monthly and check "paid" as a box rather than checking which amount they paid.
- Audience tension: The gap between "I paid the bill" and "I paid off the bill." Most people have never separated Minimum Amount Due from Total Amount Due in their own head.
- Primary engagement goal: Saves (it is a checkable decision rule) and comments (a low-stakes, common confession: "I only just realised this").
- Why people may save, share or comment: It reframes a familiar monthly action (tap pay, feel done) as a decision with a hidden cost, gives a one-line rule to check next time, and is easy to forward to a family member who "always pays something."

## Five Cover Hooks

1. Curiosity hook: Your credit card bill says paid. Your interest doesn't agree.
2. Contrarian hook: Paying on time is not the same as paying it off.
3. Relatable-pain hook: You paid something. The bank still charged you full interest.
4. Hidden-business-mechanism hook: The minimum due button is not convenience. It is a profit switch.
5. Warning-or-risk hook: Paying only the minimum can cost more than the late fee ever would.

**Selected hook:** Your credit card bill says paid. Your interest doesn't agree.

**Why it wins:** It uses a familiar, confident feeling (paid, done) and contradicts it in the same breath, which creates instant tension without sounding alarmist.

## Caption

Most people track credit card health with one signal: did I pay on time. That signal is incomplete.

Every statement carries two numbers, the Minimum Amount Due and the Total Amount Due. Clearing only the minimum keeps your account marked current with the bank and avoids a late fee. But once you miss the full amount, you can lose the interest-free period on that bill, and interest may be calculated from the date of each purchase rather than from the due date. The unpaid balance keeps collecting interest quietly while your record looks clean.

This isn't about panic. It's about knowing which number actually stops the interest. Total Amount Due does. Minimum Amount Due does not.

If your statement always shows a Minimum Due box, that box exists to make a large balance feel manageable in the short term. It isn't there to protect you from interest.

Have you ever checked which amount you were actually clearing each month, the minimum or the total?
