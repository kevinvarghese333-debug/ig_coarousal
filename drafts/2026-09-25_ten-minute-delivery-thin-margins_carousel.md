# Twenty-Six Paise

## Status

Production complete. Review before Instagram publishing.

## Strategic Premise

- Topic: The business behind 10-minute delivery, using Blinkit's first-ever profitable quarter as the concrete case.
- Central idea: Blinkit just posted its first adjusted-EBITDA-positive quarter at real scale, and it kept roughly 0.26 percent of every rupee that moved through it. Fast growth in quick commerce and a healthy, durable business are not the same thing.
- Primary audience: Urban Indian professionals who order on Blinkit, Zepto or Instamart regularly and are business-curious, plus anyone who reads "quick commerce" headlines without seeing the underlying economics.
- Audience tension: The apps feel huge, funded and permanent. The audience assumes a company this visible and well-used must already be comfortably profitable.
- Primary engagement goal: Shares and comments, since a sharp business insight like this is exactly what makes a reader look informed when they send it on.
- Why people may save, share or comment: It reframes a familiar convenience (ordering milk at midnight) as a business story with a genuinely surprising, specific number, and invites an opinion about whether the model can hold.

## Five Cover Hooks

1. Curiosity hook: "Blinkit just made its first real profit. Here is how small it was."
2. Contrarian hook: "10-minute delivery is not the success story it looks like."
3. Relatable-pain hook: "You order in 10 minutes. The company waits years to see a profit."
4. Hidden-business-mechanism hook: "This is how much quick commerce actually keeps from your order."
5. Warning-or-risk hook: "The most-funded apps on your phone are barely breaking even."

**Selected hook:** "Blinkit just made its first real profit. Here is how small it was."

**Why it wins:** It uses a specific, current, factual event as the hook rather than a general claim, which earns curiosity honestly and sets up the number reveal that follows.

## Caption

Blinkit just had its best quarter ever. It kept about 26 paise for every 100 rupees that moved through it.

That is not a typo, and it is not a failure either. It is the first time the business has been profitable at this scale, according to its parent company's latest results. The margin is just razor thin, because running 10-minute delivery means paying for a dark store near you and a rider on standby whether or not you order tonight.

A rival, Zepto, is growing even faster, and its yearly loss grew past ₹5,900 crore in the same period. Both companies are winning the growth race. Neither has fully won the profit race yet.

This is not a call to buy, sell or avoid anything. It is a reminder that a growing app and a healthy business are two different questions, and headlines usually answer only one of them.

Do you think 10-minute delivery becomes a genuinely profitable business, or a permanently subsidised convenience?
