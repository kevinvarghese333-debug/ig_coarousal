# The Interest Never Disappeared. It Moved.

## Status

Production complete. Review before Instagram publishing.

## Strategic Premise

- Topic: How "no cost EMI" actually works
- Central idea: No-cost EMI does not remove interest from your purchase. It moves the interest somewhere you cannot see it, and removes the moment where you would have asked yourself if you can afford the item.
- Primary audience: Indian salaried professionals and first-time buyers, 21 to 40, who shop online or in electronics stores and see "No Cost EMI" at checkout
- Audience tension: The checkout screen makes the purchase feel free, so the one moment they would normally pause and question the spend is the moment they stop questioning it
- Primary engagement goal: Saves and shares (a decision rule people want to reuse before their next big purchase), with comments from the closing question
- Why people may save, share or comment: It reframes a phrase they have tapped without reading, gives them one concrete question to ask a seller, and is short enough to send to a friend who is about to buy a phone or a TV on EMI

## Five Cover Hooks

1. Curiosity hook: The interest on your "no cost" EMI did not disappear. It moved.
2. Contrarian hook: No-cost EMI is not interest-free. It is interest you cannot see.
3. Relatable-pain hook: You picked EMI because the checkout screen said zero extra.
4. Hidden-business-mechanism hook: Someone always pays the interest on a "no cost" EMI. It is not the bank.
5. Warning-or-risk hook: Zero percent interest does not exist. The RBI said so in 2013.

**Selected hook:** The interest never disappeared. It moved.

**Why it wins:** It states the contrarian truth as a simple fact rather than a warning, which creates curiosity about the mechanism without fear-mongering, and it sets up a story the reader has to swipe to finish.

## Caption

"No Cost EMI" is one of the most tapped buttons in Indian retail, and one of the least understood. The zero percent framing is not a lie exactly. It is a rename. Banks do not lend for free, so when the interest line reads zero, that cost has usually moved into the price you would have paid in full, in the form of a discount you quietly gave up.

The RBI flagged this back in 2013, when it found several "0% interest" schemes were really interest dressed up as processing fees. The label has since gotten smoother. The underlying math has not changed.

The bigger cost is not even the money. It is the missing pause. Full payment forces you to ask if you can afford something today. EMI removes that question exactly when the amount is largest.

Before your next "No Cost EMI" tap, ask for the full upfront price first. Compare the two numbers. The gap is where the discount went.

Do you check the full price before choosing EMI, or does the zero percent line settle it for you?
