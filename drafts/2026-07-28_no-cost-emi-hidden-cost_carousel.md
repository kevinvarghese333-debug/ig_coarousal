# No Cost EMI Is Not the Same as Free

## Status

Production complete. Review before Instagram publishing.

## Strategic Premise

- Topic: Why "No Cost EMI" at checkout is not actually free
- Central idea: "No cost EMI" removes the interest line from your screen, not the cost. It moves into a discount you silently give up and a fee you rarely see broken down.
- Primary audience: Salaried Indian professionals, 21 to 40, who shop online and see "No Cost EMI" at checkout regularly
- Audience tension: People believe "no cost" means zero cost, then feel foolish or annoyed when they realise there was a trade-off they never evaluated
- Primary engagement goal: Saves and shares (this is a decision checklist people will want to reuse and send to someone about to buy something on EMI)
- Why people may save, share or comment: It reframes a button they tap without thinking, gives a concrete 3-point check, and invites people to compare notes on what they found when they checked

## Five Cover Hooks

1. Curiosity hook: "No cost EMI. Zero interest. So who pays the bank?"
2. Contrarian hook: "'No cost EMI' is not the same as free."
3. Relatable-pain hook: "You picked no cost EMI to save money. You may have paid more."
4. Hidden-business-mechanism hook: "The bank still charges interest on your 'no cost' EMI. Just not to you."
5. Warning-or-risk hook: "Before you tap 'No Cost EMI' at checkout, check this first."

**Selected hook:** "You picked no cost EMI to save money. You may have paid more."

**Why it wins:** It puts the reader inside a decision they have actually made, passes the "is this about me" test instantly, and creates real tension without sounding alarmist.

## Caption

"No Cost EMI" is one of the most tapped buttons in Indian online shopping, and one of the least understood. The words suggest the cost disappeared. It did not. It moved.

Banks do not waive interest as a courtesy. Under RBI's 2022 card directions, issuers must show you the principal, interest and discount that make an EMI "no cost" before you confirm it. That breakup exists precisely because the cost is real, just relabelled.

Most of us never open that screen. We compare the sticker price to the monthly number and tap confirm.

This carousel walks through where that cost actually goes: the discount you give up, and the fee that sometimes rides along on GST. None of this makes No Cost EMI a bad option. It makes it a trade, and trades are worth understanding before you make them.

Next time you see that checkout screen, have you actually compared the two prices? What did you find?
