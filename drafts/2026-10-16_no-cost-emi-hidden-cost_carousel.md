## Strategic Premise

- Topic: Zero-cost EMI
- Central idea: A no-cost EMI is not automatically free, because the interest is often offset by a discount you may have received for paying upfront.
- Primary audience: Indian salaried buyers aged 21 to 40 who buy phones and appliances on EMI
- Audience tension: The screen says ₹0 interest, so the choice feels risk free
- Primary engagement goal: Saves and comments
- Why people may save, share or comment: A three step checkout test, a relatable festive season moment, and a real judgement question

4. Hidden-business-mechanism hook: Who pays for your ₹0 interest EMI?
5. Warning-or-risk hook: Check this before you tap EMI.

**Selected hook:** “No-cost” EMI still has a price.

## Caption

Zero-cost EMI is one of the most common checkout nudges in India. The screen says ₹0 interest, the monthly number looks small, and the decision feels risk free.

Here is the part worth checking. A lender still has a cost of lending. In many no-cost EMI offers, that cost is offset by a discount from the seller or brand, and the loan may still carry a processing fee or GST on the interest. Terms differ by lender, card and seller, so read the current terms on your own offer.

The simplest test is not to ask whether there is interest. Ask for the upfront price, add up every EMI and every fee, and compare the two totals. Sometimes the totals match and the EMI is a fair convenience. Sometimes the gap is the real price of "free".

This is general education, not advice for your situation.

If a shop offered ₹3,000 off for paying today or a ₹0-interest EMI on the same phone, which would you take, and what would change your answer?
