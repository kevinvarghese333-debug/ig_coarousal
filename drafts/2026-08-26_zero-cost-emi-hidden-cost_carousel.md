# Zero-Cost EMI: Not Actually Zero

## Status

Production complete. Review before Instagram publishing.

## Strategic Premise

- Topic: What actually happens when a checkout screen offers "no-cost EMI"
- Central idea: The "no-cost EMI" button does not remove the cost of the loan. It relabels the cost as a forgone discount and a processing fee, so the checkout screen can say ₹0.
- Primary audience: Salaried, 21 to 40, buying phones, appliances or gadgets on EMI, comfortable with credit cards and UPI, not naive but rushed at checkout
- Audience tension: They want relief from feeling the interest hit right now. The button gives them that feeling without changing what actually leaves their pocket.
- Primary engagement goal: Saves (it is a decision framework people will want at their next checkout) and shares (a fact worth forwarding to a friend or family member before a big purchase)
- Why people may save, share or comment: It reframes a purchase decision people make often and rarely question. It gives them a concrete two-minute check to run next time, and a specific "did this happen to you" question that invites real answers, not just "agree?"

## Five Cover Hooks

1. Curiosity hook: The button says ₹0 extra. The receipt says otherwise.
2. Contrarian hook: Zero-cost EMI. Not actually zero.
3. Relatable-pain hook: You didn't get a discount. You lost one.
4. Hidden-business-mechanism hook: Someone always pays the interest. This time, it's still you.
5. Warning-or-risk hook: RBI already said it. Zero percent interest does not exist.

**Selected hook:** Zero-cost EMI. Not actually zero.

**Why it wins:** It is short, contrarian, and names the exact checkout moment the audience recognises without needing any setup.

## Caption

That "no cost EMI" button at checkout feels like a discovery. No interest, no catch, just smaller numbers spread across a few months. Except the cost rarely disappears. It usually moves. The upfront discount you would have gotten for paying in full quietly vanishes, and a processing fee (plus GST on it) shows up instead. RBI flagged this back in 2013, telling banks that zero percent interest is not really a thing, just interest wearing a different name.

None of this means EMI is bad. Spreading a large purchase across a few months can be a completely reasonable cash flow decision. The problem is choosing it because the checkout screen said "free" instead of because you actually did the maths.

Before your next "no-cost" purchase, compare the upfront price (with any cash discount) to the full EMI total, fees included. The gap is your real answer.

What's the last "no-cost EMI" purchase you made, and did you check what it actually cost you?
