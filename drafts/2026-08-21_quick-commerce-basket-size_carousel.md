# The 10 Minutes Was Never the Business Model

## Status

Production complete. Review before Instagram publishing.

## Strategic Premise

- Topic: The real business lever behind quick commerce (Blinkit, Zepto, Instamart, Flipkart Minutes) is average order value, not delivery speed.
- Central idea: Quick commerce apps stopped competing on how fast they deliver. A dark store costs roughly the same to run whether your order is small or large, so the number that actually decides profitability is how much you buy per order, and the fee prompts and add-on nudges exist to move that number up.
- Primary audience: Salaried, 21 to 40, urban, regular users of Blinkit, Zepto or Instamart for small, frequent top-up purchases.
- Audience tension: The 10-minute delivery feels like a personal convenience win. It is actually a customer-acquisition hook for a business whose real optimisation target is the size of your basket, not the speed of your order.
- Primary engagement goal: Comments and shares. This is a "wait, that explains the fee prompt" carousel, built to make people recognise their own ordering behaviour and compare notes.
- Why people may save, share or comment: It reframes a near-daily action (a small quick commerce order) with a specific, verifiable business mechanism most users have never thought about, and ends with a comment question almost everyone has a personal answer to.

## Five Cover Hooks

1. Curiosity hook: The 10-minute race is basically over. Nobody told you what replaced it.
2. Contrarian hook: The 10 minutes was never the business model.
3. Relatable-pain hook: You ordered one item. The app charged you for being small.
4. Hidden-business-mechanism hook: Quick commerce apps don't make money on your 10 minutes. They make money on what you add after.
5. Warning-or-risk hook: That "add more to avoid a fee" prompt is not a suggestion. It is the business model.

**Selected hook:** The 10 minutes was never the business model.

**Why it wins:** It is a direct, contrarian correction of something almost every reader has personally believed (that speed is the product), which creates instant tension without needing a warning tone, and sets up a mechanism (basket size, not delivery time) the rest of the carousel can prove.

## Caption

11 pm. One packet of chips. Delivered in nine minutes. It feels like a small miracle, not a business decision. It is both.

Quick commerce did not build a ten-minute delivery network because speed is where the money is. A dark store costs roughly the same to run whether your order is worth 100 rupees or 500. Rent, staff and inventory do not shrink for a small basket. The number that actually decides whether a platform makes money is average order value, not delivery time.

That is why the more persistent prompt on these apps is not "arriving in 9 minutes." It is "add more to avoid a fee." Speed gets you to open the app. Basket size is what keeps the business open.

None of this makes the convenience fake. It is real, and often worth paying for. But the next time an app nudges you to add one more item, it is worth a two-second pause: would you have bought this anyway?

What is the smallest thing you have added to a cart just to avoid a fee?

## Design Handoff

- Canvas: 1080 x 1350 px, RGB, one PNG per slide.
- Palette: Navy `#080C18` background throughout. Gold `#C9A84C` for accents, kickers, highlighted figures and card borders. Off-white `#F6F1E7` for primary text. Slate `#AEB7C2` for secondary labels and source notes. No red or green needed; the story has no danger signal or positive-outcome beat that calls for them.
- Typography: Editorial serif for headlines and the "9 MIN" hero figure. Clean sans serif for body copy, labels, slide counters and source notes. Two typefaces only.
- Image or screenshot requirements: None. Every visual element is code-rendered (timer card, dark-store icon, bar chart, cart icon, checklist card, comparison bars). No real app screenshots are used, since simulating a specific app's interface would misrepresent it.
- Chart and data guidance: One clear bar chart on Slide 5 (average order value by platform), one simple two-bar comparison on Slide 9. No dense charts. Source cited in small type on Slide 5.
- Mobile-readability notes: Headline sizes roughly 80 to 110 px, subheads 36 to 48 px, body 30 to 38 px. Safe margins of at least 80 px on all sides. Nothing critical within 60 px of any edge.
- Accessibility notes: Off-white on navy gives very high contrast for body text. Gold is reserved for large text, numerals and outlines rather than small body copy. No meaning is conveyed by colour alone; every highlighted element also carries a text label.
- Animation notes: Static carousel. No slide requires motion to be understood, so no video slides were produced.
